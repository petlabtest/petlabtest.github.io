Accelerating Basic Science

Scientific breakthroughs often begin with a new ability to observe. When molecular migration within living organisms can be continuously measured, when transient events can be digitally recorded, and when detectors can be reconfigured around problems rather than fixed products, all-digital PET is more than just a medical device; it becomes an experimental platform connecting physics, life sciences, medicine, pharmacology, and computational science.
September 9, 2026
Digital PET Laboratory
Basic science requires answering mechanistic questions that occur in the interwoven processes of real life involving space, time, and scale. The uniqueness of PET lies in combining radioactive tracer principles with tomographic imaging to allow researchers to observe specific molecular dynamics without destroying the object. All-digital PET further entrusts raw events to computation, transforming the instrument from a 'black box that produces images' into one that can continuously raise new questions as a measurement system.

PET Establishes Temporal and Molecular Coordinates for Living Processes

Many life science questions cannot be answered by fixed sections alone. Tumor glucose metabolism changes with hypoxia and treatment, neurotransmitter release occurs during behavior, immune cells migrate between organs, drugs reach their targets but are also metabolized in the liver and kidneys. Histology provides cellular-level details but often requires sampling or even termination of experiments; blood tests can sample continuously but lack whole-body spatial information; structural imaging shows anatomical changes but may not be sensitive to early molecular events.

PET uses tracers to encode a biological question into detectable radionuclides. Glucose analogs reflect glucose metabolism, specific ligands can target receptors, enzymes, or abnormal proteins, and labeled drugs can show distribution in the body. The detector measures not abstract 'brightness' but decay events from the radionuclide; after corrections for attenuation, scattering, random coincidences, and system response, event statistics can be converted into activity concentration and kinetic parameters.

'Non-invasive' enables longitudinal studies. The same individual can be imaged repeatedly during disease onset, treatment intervention, and recovery, thereby converting inter-individual differences into intra-individual changes. Small animal research can reduce the number of animals needed for different time points; clinical research can observe patients' true physiological processes. This temporal continuity is especially important for development, aging, nutrition, exercise, and chronic diseases.

'Quantitative' enables PET to go beyond visual interpretation. Standard uptake values in static images allow semi-quantitative comparisons; dynamic acquisitions combined with blood input functions and compartment models can estimate tracer transport from blood to tissue, irreversible uptake rates, distribution volumes, or receptor binding potential. Parameter models may not fit all tissues, but they provide a mathematical language for transforming images into testable physiological hypotheses.

'Whole-body' makes inter-organ relationships observable. Immune responses, endocrine functions, metabolism, and drug effects are not single-organ events. If different organs are acquired at different bed positions or times, rapid processes cannot be directly compared; if a long-axis field-of-view system simultaneously covers major organs, researchers can establish time-activity curves on the same temporal baseline to study the sequence of signals entering the liver, brain, muscle, tumor, and excretion systems from the blood pool.

Another value of PET is its translatability. The same or similar tracer principles can be applied from cell experiments and small animal models to human studies, with changes in instrument scale and dose but continuity in the problem chain. Researchers can first validate targets, then study pharmacokinetics, and finally assess target expression and treatment response in patients. This cross-scale pathway is crucial for new radiopharmaceuticals, drugs, and integrated diagnosis and therapy.

However, the scientific value of PET is constrained by measurement conditions. Injectable activity is limited, short-lived isotopes decay rapidly, small structures exhibit partial volume effects, respiration and motion cause blurring, low-count time frames have high noise levels, tracer specificity and metabolites affect interpretation. Scientific Breakthroughs Require PET to Deliver Precise, Rapid, and Sustained Observation

Low-Dose and Rare-Target Studies Depend on Weak-Signal and Longitudinal Observation

Some receptors have low density, some cell populations are sparse, and certain drugs only allow microdosing. Research on children and healthy volunteers requires minimizing radiation exposure. These scenarios collectively require high effective sensitivity: the system must collect more true coincidences, suppress random and scattered backgrounds, and provide stable estimates at low counts. If increased sensitivity is used merely to achieve smoother images without entering experimental design, its scientific potential remains unfulfilled.

Delayed imaging presents another challenge for weak signals. Large molecule probes, antibodies, and partial receptor ligands may require extended time to achieve good target-to-background ratios, but the radioisotope decays continuously. Traditional systems lack sufficient counts at later time points. More sensitive systems can extend the observation window, study slow kinetics and clearance processes, and provide better tools for immune PET and long-half-life isotopes.

Temporal Resolution Reveals Transport, Exchange, and Feedback

Dynamic PET breaks acquisition into consecutive time frames. Early seconds to minutes post-injection reflect blood flow and rapid transport; subsequent stages show tissue uptake, metabolism, binding, and clearance. Shorter time frames mean fewer counts per frame; fewer counts lead to less stable parameter estimates. High sensitivity, accurate timing measurements, and statistical reconstruction must work in concert to convert 'fast shooting' into credible kinetics.

For neuroscience, brief stimuli, task switching, and neurotransmitter release may require higher temporal resolution; for cardiovascular research, blood flow varies during the cardiac and respiratory cycles; for drug studies, first-pass effects may only last a short time. Scientific questions demand that the system retain event timestamps and allow researchers to adjust frame width, gating, and models post-acquisition rather than fixing them permanently at the time of acquisition.

Spatial Resolution Must Balance Small Structures, Field Uniformity, and Penetration Depth

Spatial resolution is determined by crystal size, positron range, non-collinearity, detector geometry, depth of penetration, and reconstruction. Simply reducing the crystal size does not infinitely improve resolution: light collection becomes more difficult, channel count and data volume increase, and parallax errors occur with off-axis incidence away from the center of the field of view. Brain imaging, small animal studies, plant root systems, and cellular pathology require detector redesign for specific targets rather than scaling down clinical whole-body systems proportionally.

Uniformity across the entire field of view is equally important. An instrument that performs well only at a central point may not be suitable for quantitative research. Dedicated brain PET needs to cover both cortical and deep structures, small animal systems must accommodate various postures, and long-axis systems need to control axial response variations. Research breakthroughs require spatial resolution, sensitivity, and quantification errors to be measured and reported throughout the target region.

Natural-State Imaging Reduces the Effects of Anesthesia, Restraint, and Motion Artifacts

Traditional brain PET requires subjects to remain stationary, while small animals often need anesthesia. Anesthesia and restraint alter neural, metabolic, and cardiovascular states, deviating the research subject from natural behavior. Helmet-style detectors, motion tracking, and event-level motion correction may bring PET into an active state; dual-dynamic small animal systems attempt to simultaneously record brain activity and whole-body physiology in animals. Such studies demand lightweight mechanical structures, real-time geometry, synchronization, and new algorithms.

Open Architectures Bring New Algorithms into Real Instruments

Frontier problems often lack established protocols. Researchers need to modify time windows, try new scatter models, jointly estimate motion and activity, construct direct parameter reconstruction, or place artificial intelligence in pulse processing rather than final image stages. If equipment only outputs closed-format final images, these attempts cannot be made. Raw events, geometric models, calibration data, and programmable workflows thus become part of the research infrastructure.

Reproducible Research Converts Compelling Images into Reliable Evidence

Scientific research must know how images are generated. Acquisition time, injected activity, residual dose, reconstruction iterations, filtering, corrections, motion processing, and software versions can all affect results. New-generation PET needs to automatically save metadata, support data traceability, and establish measurement reliability through standard phantoms, day-to-day stability, and cross-center comparisons. Performance improvements without uncertainty assessments may instead create more confident biases.

All-Digital PET Redistributes Control over Imaging Information

Traditional PET has evolved into a mature and reliable clinical technology, with modern commercial systems widely adopting SiPMs, TOF, and advanced iterative reconstruction techniques. Describing 'traditional' as outdated is inaccurate. The core difference of all-digital PET does not lie in whether individual components are advanced but rather in whether the original scintillation pulses are precisely digitized at their source, whether single-event information is retained, and whether detector and processing workflows can be reconfigured by software.

In analog-digital hybrid chains, the analog front-end rapidly forms event parameters through shaping, integration, and discrimination processes. This approach is efficient but many decisions are irreversible. In all-digital paths, methods like MVT record pulse information, which is then used by digital algorithms to estimate time and energy. It shifts some complexity from fixed circuits to upgradable software, allowing problems previously determined during the manufacturing phase to be re-evaluated in the research stage.

This shift is particularly important for fundamental research. Researchers can preserve multiple characteristics of the same event, compare pulse models, identify pile-ups and anomalous waveforms; they can reorganize coincidences from single events, change time windows and energy windows; correlate detector states with image errors; and reprocess old experiments when new algorithms emerge. Data transitions from a one-time intermediate product to a sustainable research object.

All-digital PET is not without its costs. Event-level data volumes are enormous; long-axis systems require higher throughput and storage capacity; software freedom increases validation combinations; algorithms dependent on incorrect models will yield biased digital results; channel thresholds, temperature, and clock deviations need precise calibration. It offers a higher information ceiling and more flexible research pathways rather than 'automatic correctness' independent of experimental conditions.

Digitalization, SiPMs, TOF, and long-axis field-of-view should be understood separately. SiPM improves compactness, magnetic compatibility, and timing potential; TOF utilizes time-of-flight differences to enhance response line localization; a long-axis field-of-view increases geometric sensitivity and supports synchronous multi-organ imaging; an all-digital architecture preserves and organizes event information. Excellent systems often combine these capabilities, but scientific papers should specify which layer provides the specific benefits.

Five Technical Pathways in All-Digital PET Produce Five Forms of Scientific Evidence

High-Fidelity Pulse Data Supports Detector-Physics Research

The process of scintillation light generation, propagation, and conversion by SiPMs includes event time, energy, interaction location, and detector status. Digital sampling allows researchers to establish waveform libraries, compare different crystals, optoelectronic devices, bias voltages, temperatures, and coupling methods. Studies on pile-up pulses, dark counts, optical crosstalk, and saturation do not have to rely solely on the final energy spectrum but can return to event morphology for causes.

This type of data also drives three-dimensional position encoding. The interaction depth of gamma rays in crystals affects oblique incidence localization; fine crystal arrays carry depth information through light distribution patterns. If detectors retain multi-channel intensity and time characteristics, statistical models or machine learning can estimate three-dimensional positions. Whether ultra-fine coding translates into system resolution still needs validation under full field-of-view and realistic count rates.

Accurate Timing Supports Rapid, Low-Count, and Dynamic Experiments

TOF does not directly measure the annihilation points but adds probabilistic constraints to each response line. The stronger the constraint, the less dependent the reconstruction is on distant irrelevant events, resulting in a higher effective signal-to-noise ratio under low-count conditions. Researchers can use this benefit for shorter frame widths to observe first-pass perfusion; lower activity levels for longitudinal studies; or finer voxels to study small structures.

Synchronization error must be measured and data interfaces must remain open; otherwise, “simultaneous acquisition” does not amount to interpretable alignment.

Modular Detectors Support Geometries Tailored to Scientific Questions

Objects in basic research vary enormously. Plant branches and leaves do not fit naturally inside a ring; studies of freely behaving small animals need open space; proton beams require a clear passage between detectors; brain imaging benefits from placing detectors close to the head; and intraoperative imaging must avoid the surgical field. Modular all-digital detectors can form panels, curved surfaces, partial rings, and reconfigurable structures, allowing the application to determine the geometry.

Custom geometries introduce incomplete angular coverage, nonuniform sensitivity, and difficult calibration. The value of an all-digital architecture is that it describes geometry and channel status explicitly to the reconstruction algorithm while retaining event-level information for model-based compensation. A specialized system does not gain an advantage merely by sacrificing generality; it must demonstrate a quantifiable performance benefit for its intended task.

Open Software Supports Algorithm Validation and Interdisciplinary Experiments

PnI separates imaging protocols, algorithm modules, and device descriptions into layers, allowing pulse processing, coincidence processing, correction, reconstruction, and display to be combined through standard interfaces. Electronics researchers can optimize event estimation, mathematicians can study inverse problems, computer scientists can validate parallel and AI algorithms, and medical researchers can define interpretable endpoints. Shared data semantics reduce the costly “translation loss” of interdisciplinary collaboration.

Open-source software also allows the establishment of benchmarks. The same raw data can be reconstructed using different algorithms, and the same algorithm can be tested on different geometries; errors can then be compared with known phantom ground truths. If parameters, code versions, random seeds, and calibration files are saved alongside these reconstructions, PET algorithm research can move from demonstrating individual cases to reproducible comparisons.

A Complete Innovation Chain Connects Methods with Real-World Scenarios

Basic research instruments often stagnate at the 'works once' prototype stage. The Digital PET Laboratory connects critical materials, low-light components, detectors, systems, software, and applications, meaning anomalies in experiments can be traced along the chain: image artifacts may originate from reconstruction or from crystal consistency, threshold drift, synchronization, or mechanical geometry. Cross-layer teams can more quickly identify bottlenecks, and application feedback can return to detector design.

Collaboration with hospital and industry partners places research questions within real-world constraints. Patient workflows demand speed and reliability; clinical trials require safety and statistical evidence; production requires consistency; regulations mandate traceability. After undergoing these constraints, scientific instruments are more likely to form stable shared platforms; at the same time, clinical issues will also propose new fundamental research topics in reverse, such as motion correction, low-dose quantification, and early evaluation of treatment response.

All-Digital PET May Advance Breakthroughs across Multiple Scientific Fields

Mapping Dynamic Molecular Networks Between Human Organs

Long-axis whole-body dynamic PET may advance system physiology from concept to human measurement. Researchers can simultaneously observe the time courses of glucose, fatty acids, amino acids, or immune probes in multiple organs and construct models of delays, coupling, and feedback between organs. It could help explain how tumors alter systemic metabolism, how myocardial injury triggers an immune response, and how inflammation propagates along the bone marrow–spleen–lesion axis.

True breakthroughs will not rely solely on correlation networks. Organ time curves are influenced by blood flow, volume, metabolites, and partial volumes; they must be combined with physiological models, intervention experiments, and independent biomarkers for causal interpretation. All-digital PET provides synchronized data, but rigorous design is needed for causal explanations.

Investigating Brain-Body Coupling in Natural Behaviors

Combining helmet-style PET, dual-dynamic small-animal PET, and high-precision motion correction may enable the study of brain metabolism's coordination with peripheral organs during movement, learning, social interaction, and stress. For Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, depression, and neuroimmune disorders, understanding the relationship between brain changes and the gut, immune, and endocrine systems is a critical frontier.

Wearable does not equate to completely non-intrusive. Device weight, cables, field of view, behavioral environment, and tracer kinetics can all impact experiments. Future work will require integrating behavioral science, neuroscience, mechanical engineering, and imaging physics into protocols, and openly reporting motion correction failures and data exclusion criteria.

Accelerating the 'From Target to Human' Pathway for New Tracers and Drugs

High-sensitivity low-dose imaging can support microdosing first-in-human studies, observing candidate drugs’ systemic distribution, target organ entry and clearance at lower risk. Delayed imaging may serve slow kinetics probes, while multi-tracer sequences could compare different metabolic or receptor pathways in a single study. Earlier access to human pharmacokinetic evidence offers opportunities to eliminate unsuitable candidates and optimize dosing.

This still relies on radiochemistry, toxicology, quality control, and regulatory compliance. PET cannot salvage probes lacking specificity nor replace all efficacy and safety testing with imaging. Its contribution is transforming previously invisible in vivo distribution into quantifiable evidence.

Enabling In-Beam 'Range Eyes' for Proton and Heavy-Ion Therapy

In-beam PET, if capable of reliably identifying therapy-induced radionuclides and correlating activity distributions with planned doses, tissue composition, and beam log data, may provide range verification within or between fractions. Future research could advance from 'whether there is a shift' to identifying error sources and adaptive treatment decisions, allowing the physical advantages of particle therapy to be more reliably realized.

The activity measured by PET does not equate to dose itself. Nuclear reaction cross-sections, elemental composition, biological washout, and detector geometry all influence this relationship. Breakthroughs require validation through Monte Carlo simulations, nuclear data, medical physics, and clinical outcomes; a single beautiful image cannot substitute for dosimetric evidence.

Revealing Dynamic Patterns of Plant Carbon Allocation and Stress Responses

Plant PET can observe the transport of photosynthetic products from leaves to roots, fruits, and new tissues using tracers related to carbon, nitrogen, or water. The all-digital variable structure enhances adaptability to different plant morphologies, and dynamic quantification may reveal resource allocation strategies under drought, salt stress, pathogen infection, and nutrient changes, providing in vivo evidence for crop breeding and ecological models.

Plant scale, water content, movement, and environmental control differ from medical subjects, and tracer preparation and laboratory radiation conditions also present barriers. Breakthroughs in this direction will come from plant physiologists and instrument teams defining the problem together rather than directly transplanting medical protocols.

Establishing Cross-Scale, Multimodal Digital-Twin Experiments

Event-level PET data can be combined with CT, MRI, optical imaging, ultrasound, genomics, and electronic health records to form multimodal models from molecular to organ levels. Dynamic parameters can constrain physiological simulations, while model predictions guide the next acquisition time and tracer selection. All-digital PET serves as a quantitative sensor rather than the sole information source here.

"Digital twin" has scientific significance only when measurement errors, model assumptions, and external validation are clearly defined. High-dimensional data is prone to overfitting, and cross-center data distributions can vary. Research should pre-register key endpoints, retain independent validation sets, and report under which conditions the model fails.

Exploring New Methods for High-Energy Particles and Distributed Radiation Observation

MVT addresses the general problem of high-speed pulse digitization. The Digital PET team's public documentation has proposed using correlation detection methods for nuclear radiation, security checks, logging, and even distributed observations of cosmic ray events. Modular detectors and networked time synchronization may organize multiple nodes into large-scale observation systems to study rare particle events or complex radiation fields.

This is one of the most exploratory directions. Mature crystals, electronics, and coincidence concepts in medical PET can provide a starting point, but cosmic ray energy spectra, background, triggering, and environmental reliability are entirely different. Any 'possibility' must be gradually transformed into evidence through specialized detector design, open data, and cross-validation with existing high-energy physics facilities.

Scientific instruments do not automatically generate scientific discoveries.All-digital PET expands the observable range; radiotracers determine the observed subjects; experimental design establishes causal pathways; models convert counts into parameters; and clinical and biological teams complete the interpretation. So-called 'technical support' is about enabling these stages to work in harmony with higher-quality data, rather than using instruments to replace scientific judgment.

Methodology, Data Governance, and Research Communities Jointly Accelerate Discovery

The first task is to establish a language for comparable performance. Spatial resolution, sensitivity, count rate, scatter fraction, TOF (time-of-flight), and quantitative accuracy must be measured according to standard methods and reported with reconstruction and object conditions. New geometries require task-oriented evaluations, such as proton range error, regional brain quantification bias, and plant transport parameter reproducibility. Only when 'imaging capability' is translated into 'how much error in the target mission' does an instrument truly enter science.

The second task is to preserve complete metadata. Raw events cannot be reproduced without thresholds, temperature, geometry, time correction, injection records, and acquisition logs. Data formats should serve both long-term archival storage and high-performance computing; sensitive human data must be anonymized, access controlled, and reviewed for intended use. Openness does not mean uploading all data unconditionally but providing verifiable evidence within ethical and intellectual property boundaries.

The third task is to establish a chain of algorithm validation. Denoising, direct reconstruction, and parameter estimation must be tested across multiple levels: simulation, phantoms, animals, and humans; AI models should evaluate cross-device and cross-center generalization; any algorithms that may affect clinical judgment should retain input, version, and uncertainty records. The all-digital platform brings algorithms into earlier signal stages, thus increasing validation responsibility.

The fourth task is to share instruments and talent. PET research spans nuclear chemistry, physics, electronics, mechanics, computation, medicine, and statistics; a single team cannot independently complete the entire chain. Sharing detector modules, standard phantoms, software interfaces, tracer platforms, and clinical cohorts can reduce redundant construction; joint training of students enables researchers to understand both their own specialty and how errors propagate in adjacent stages.

The fifth task is to allow negative results into the knowledge system. New tracers may lack specificity, new geometries may introduce edge-of-field bias, and new algorithms may only be effective on single-center data. Publicly disclosing boundaries and failure conditions prevents other teams from incurring repeated costs and helps future systems improve. The highest value of digitization is not generating more data but making the evidence chain more transparent.

The 'acceleration' of basic science progress should not be measured solely by paper output or scan speed. True acceleration means shortening the cycle from posing a question to obtaining falsifiable data, enabling one precious experiment to answer more pre-defined questions, allowing different institutions to replicate results, and re-reading historical data with technological iterations. All-digital PET provides the foundation for these changes.

All-Digital PET Research Converts Scientific Questions into Credible Conclusions

Formulate the Research Objective as a Falsifiable Hypothesis

If researchers aim to determine whether an immunotherapy alters immune activity between tumors and the spleen or bone marrow before efficacy is observed, the project should not merely state 'Use advanced PET to observe immunity.' Instead, it must specify tracer targets, expected change directions, time windows, primary organs, control groups, and minimal clinically meaningful effects. Only then can the instrument team determine the necessary coverage, frame width, counts, and quantitative repeatability.

The scientific question also determines the tracer. Whether the target is specific, whether the tracer crosses membranes, whether radioactive metabolites enter tissues, and whether the half-life of the radionuclide matches biological processes will limit experiments earlier than scanner performance alone. The radiopharmaceutical team should collaborate with imaging teams to complete in vitro binding, stability, and preliminary biodistribution studies to avoid high-performance devices precisely measuring incorrect targets.

Use Simulation and Bench Testing to Define Appropriate Performance Requirements

Monte Carlo simulations can estimate object attenuation, scattering, random coincidences, and effective counts for different geometries. Digital phantoms can test reconstruction biases. Bench tests revalidate crystal, SiPM, MVT threshold, and time synchronization. Simulations are not a substitute for reality but can quickly eliminate obviously unfeasible solutions and link system metrics to biological effects.

For example, short-term dynamic studies may prioritize sensitivity and temporal resolution, while microstructural brain nuclei research emphasizes spatial resolution and penetration depth; proton monitoring requires open geometry, beam synchronization, and high background handling. Different tasks require different 'best PET.' The value of all-digital modular systems lies in selecting the most appropriate trade-offs on a single technological platform.

Separate Random Errors from Systematic Biases in Phantom Studies

Standard phantoms provide known dimensions, activity levels, and background to test recovery coefficients, uniformity, and count rates; task-specific phantoms simulate particular brain regions, motion, plant structures, or proton ranges. Researchers should repeat scans with varying activities and positions to check if results hold only under one 'best-looking' setting. All-digital data allows tracing deviations back to channels and events.

At this stage, the initial analysis protocol must be frozen. Changing window levels, iteration counts, and filters after seeing results can lead to selecting an accidentally optimal image. Exploratory parameter tuning can be retained, but confirmatory experiments should predefine primary procedures and report other processing as sensitivity analyses.

Re-Evaluate Scale When Moving from Animal to Human Studies

Animal models can validate targeting and mechanisms but cannot automatically predict human pharmacokinetics, immune system responses, or disease heterogeneity. The spatial resolution of small-animal PET, anesthesia, body temperature, and relative injected dose differ from those in humans. Before transitioning to human studies, it is necessary to reassess radiation dosimetry, blood sampling, scan tolerance, motion, and statistical power; one cannot simply scale up animal protocols.

Low-dose capability is attractive for first-in-human studies, but the ability of a device to detect signals does not automatically establish ethical research. Risks to participants, alternative methods, handling incidental findings, data use, and exit strategies must be clearly defined. Strict risk-benefit arguments are especially necessary for healthy volunteers and children.

Treat Kinetic Models as Testable Hypotheses

Compartmental models simplify complex physiological processes into several states and rate constants. Model selection, input functions, metabolite correction, time delays, and parameter identifiability can influence conclusions. Whole-body dynamic data include multiple tissues, but different organs may not be suitable for the same model. Researchers should compare alternative models, report fitting residuals and parameter uncertainties, and validate key interpretations with independent physiological measurements.

Artificial intelligence can accelerate parametric image generation, but training objectives must align with physical measurements. If high-dose images are used as 'ground truth,' the network may learn specific reconstruction styles rather than true activity; if training data come from a single device, cross-center applications may be biased. Event-level data offer opportunities for more physically consistent models and require stricter data segmentation and external validation.

Enable Independent Teams to Reproduce the Study

A reproducible study should at least specify equipment version, axial field of view, tracer synthesis and injection, acquisition start and end times, time frames, corrections, motion handling, reconstruction parameters, region definitions, kinetic models, and statistical plans. If data cannot be publicly shared, digital phantoms, partially anonymized data, or code that can run in a controlled environment should be made available. Method transparency is as important as image quality.

Multi-center reproducibility is key to testing scientific value. Different devices, radiotracer batches, participant populations, and operators may expose single-center vulnerabilities not previously identified. All-digital PET can reduce these differences through standardized equipment descriptions and data interfaces, accelerating a one-time experiment into a method usable by the community.

Feed Results Back into Instrument Development and the Next Hypothesis Cycle

Failures in scientific applications often point toward the direction for the next generation of instruments: motion causing bias drives real-time tracking; low uptake targets with insufficient counts drive longer axial fields of view; models unable to distinguish rapid processes drive more accurate time stamps; specialized geometry edge non-uniformity drives three-dimensional position encoding. An all-digital platform allows these feedbacks to trace back to original events, forming a 'problem-instrument-data-model-new problem' closed loop.

Therefore, acceleration does not mean skipping steps but rather reducing irreversible information loss and redundant construction. Each stage should leave reusable data, code, modules, and knowledge so that subsequent research need not start from scratch; each conclusion report should specify its applicable boundaries so that the next team knows where to continue. The deepest scientific advantage of all-digital PET is embedding learning capabilities into the instrument.

An explanation mechanism for the public and research participants should also be established. Terms such as 'low dose', 'dynamic', 'whole-body', and 'artificial intelligence reconstruction' in basic research have strict technical meanings, and the research team needs to clarify what questions the examination can and cannot answer, whether individual images are diagnostically meaningful, and how incidental findings should be handled. The understanding and trust of participants in the study process is the foundation for long-term cohorts and repeated measurements.

Ultimately, scientific value must be proven through subsequent use. A new method that can only be run by its inventing team is still not a mature infrastructure; high-dimensional data without generating new questions from other disciplines has not reached its full potential. All-digital PET should lower entry barriers with modules, data, and training to enable more researchers to independently formulate hypotheses, verify results, and in turn improve the instrument. The best way for technology to support science is by giving new observational capabilities to the entire community.

This article distinguishes between systems and research progress reported in publicly available documents, technical capabilities supported by peer-reviewed literature, and future scientific directions that remain to be validated. The latter are described using conditional terms such as 'possible' and 'promising', and should not be interpreted as already established scientific discoveries or clinical conclusions.
