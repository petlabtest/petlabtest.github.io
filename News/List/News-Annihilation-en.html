The Light of Annihilation

Annihilation: a positron meets an electron, matter vanishes, mass turns into energy, and a pair of 511 keV photons is born. It took humanity nearly a century to understand where this light comes from: Dirac predicted antimatter in an equation, Anderson found the positron in cosmic rays, scintillation crystals made it stop, the photoelectric effect turned it into electrical signals, and all-digital PET turned those signals into data and images. From the prophecy of antimatter to single-photon detection, this journey spans two revolutions in twentieth-century physics and a technology that changed medicine.

At some instant in the universe, a positron meets an electron. In the moment of contact, both vanish, and their entire mass turns into energy: a pair of photons of exactly 511 keV, flying in opposite directions. This light born of annihilation travels out of Dirac's equation, through cloud-chamber photographs, scintillation crystals, photodetectors, and digital circuits, until it becomes an image before a physician's eyes. It is the most invisible light, and the most honest one: every positron annihilation leaves a coordinate in the universe that can be measured.

The Prophecy and Discovery of Antimatter

The ghost of an equation: Dirac and antimatter

In 1928, Dirac set out to unify quantum mechanics with special relativity and wrote down the equation that now bears his name. The equation unexpectedly admitted two families of solutions: one corresponding to the real electron, the other to a 'ghost' state of negative energy. In classical physics, negative-energy states imply instability; electrons should keep falling to lower energies and the entire atomic world should collapse.

Dirac's resolution was bold and strange: a vacuum is not empty but a 'sea' filled with negative-energy electrons. A hole in that sea would behave like a particle with the electron's mass and the opposite charge. No experiment had ever seen such a particle; it was a conclusion forced out by the equation itself. In 1931, Dirac called it the 'anti-electron.' A purely theoretical prophecy, placed before experimental physics.

A track in the cloud chamber: Anderson discovers the positron

In 1932, while studying cosmic rays, the American physicist Anderson photographed a peculiar track in a cloud chamber: it curved in the opposite direction from an electron, yet with the same degree of curvature. Anderson first took it for a proton, but the curvature showed it was far lighter. It was a particle with the electron's mass and the opposite charge. Anderson named it the 'positron.'

Remarkably, Anderson did not know Dirac's prophecy at the time. The ghost particle reasoned out by a theorist and the track stumbled upon by an experimentalist met on the same particle. In 1936, at the age of thirty-one, Anderson received the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

Pair production and annihilation radiation: the prophecy fully confirmed

In 1933, Blackett and Occhialini observed in a cloud chamber that a high-energy photon can turn into a pair of electrons, one positive and one negative, matter born from energy, exactly as Dirac's theory required. In the same year, Thibaud and, independently, the Joliot-Curies observed the distinctive radiation emitted when a positron meets an electron: a pair of photons of exactly 511 keV. It was humanity's first direct sight of the light of annihilation.

With that, Dirac's prophecy of four years earlier was fully confirmed: antimatter exists, particle and antiparticle annihilate on contact, mass turns into energy, and a characteristic light is emitted. A discovery that began in an equation and ended in a cloud chamber became one of the most dramatic chapters of twentieth-century physics.

When Matter Vanishes, Light Is Born

Annihilation: a farewell between particle and antiparticle

Annihilation is one of the most complete events in particle physics: a particle meets its antiparticle, both disappear, and their mass and kinetic energy are entirely converted into energy. The most common and cleanest case is electron-positron annihilation, e⁺ + e⁻ → 2γ. In a broad sense, every particle has its antiparticle; a proton and an antiproton, a neutron and an antineutron, annihilate just the same. But electron-positron annihilation is the purest in energy and momentum, and the most widely used in medicine and materials science.

Why exactly 511 keV

Mass and energy are connected by E = mc². The electron's rest mass is about 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, and electron-positron annihilation releases a total energy of 2mₑc², about 1.022 MeV. Energy conservation requires this energy to be carried away by photons: if two photons are produced, each carries exactly mₑc², about 511 keV; a single photon would require a third participant to recoil, in order to satisfy momentum conservation at the same time.

511 keV is therefore the 'fingerprint' of the light of annihilation: whether in a human body, in a material, or deep in the universe, the light of electron-positron annihilation always carries this energy mark. Detecting it is equivalent to confirming that an annihilation has occurred.

Why always in opposite pairs

Momentum conservation determines the directions of the two photons. In the center-of-mass frame, the initial momenta of the positron and electron sum to zero, so the two final photons must have equal and opposite momenta, each carrying 511 keV/c. This pair of opposite photons carries the location of the annihilation: they leave together and fly in opposite directions along the same line.

If a detector can capture both photons at once, the annihilation point is pinned down somewhere on that line. On this simple geometric fact rests the entire physical foundation of positron emission tomography (PET).

Where do positrons come from
Annihilation comes after the positron, not before. Neutron-rich nuclides in PET tracers (such as F-18) emit positrons through β⁺ decay; the positron slows down in tissue, drifts about one to two millimeters, and only then meets an electron and annihilates. Each annihilation point in a PET image therefore approximates the position of one nuclear decay, and the positron range between them is one of the physical limits on spatial resolution.

Making the Light of Annihilation Stop

The most invisible light

A 511 keV photon carries enormous energy and penetration. The naked eye, lenses, and even semiconductor sensors cannot capture it directly: its interaction probability with detector material is very low, so direct conversion is hopelessly inefficient, while the energy it deposits can disrupt ordinary circuits. To make this light speak, a different path is required.

Detector scientists chose 'stop first, convert second': first make the annihilation photon stop, turn it into easier-to-handle visible light, then measure it electronically. This is scintillation detection, a complete chain that turns the invisible into the visible and energy into data.

Scintillation crystals: turning gamma photons into visible light

The job of a scintillation crystal is to convert the energy of a 511 keV photon efficiently into visible light. The LYSO (lutetium yttrium oxyorthosilicate) crystal widely used in today's PET detectors has high density, a large atomic number, and fast, high-yield scintillation: a single absorbed 511 keV photon can excite tens of thousands of blue-violet visible photons inside the crystal.

When these visible photons fly out of the crystal, they carry all the information that 'a beam of annihilation light was here,' waiting for a photodetector to read them.

The photoelectric effect: turning light into electricity

Visible photons strike the photodetector and, through the photoelectric effect, become electrons. The rule Einstein wrote down in 1905, E = hν, here becomes an engineering fact: the photocathode of a photomultiplier tube uses the external photoelectric effect to turn a photon into an electron, and dynode stages multiply it step by step, one photon ultimately becoming a million electrons; a silicon photomultiplier (SiPM) uses the internal photoelectric effect to excite electron-hole pairs in a semiconductor and then amplifies them by Geiger avalanche.

A signal so faint it barely exists is amplified by a factor of 100,000 to 1,000,000, and the information carried by light becomes an electrical signal. Humanity's ability to 'see' any invisible light rests on the rule Einstein wrote down in 1905.

All-digital readout: turning electricity into data

Traditional analog-digital hybrid PET amplifies, shapes, and discriminates pulses in the analog front end, discarding a great deal of information before entering the computer. The multi-voltage threshold (MVT) sampling proposed by the Digital PET Laboratory changes this: it no longer asks 'what is the voltage now' at uniform clock intervals; instead, it records when a pulse crosses multiple voltage thresholds, and then uses pulse models to recover time, energy, and waveform.

Scintillation pulses are precisely digitized at their source, and every annihilation event becomes rows of time and energy records, with the raw information preserved, traceable, and reprocessable.

How the Light of Annihilation Sees the World

Coincidence detection: locating the annihilation

PET's ring of detectors waits simultaneously for two photons flying in opposite directions. If two photons arrive almost at the same time, the detector assumes they come from the same annihilation and records the event on the line (the line of response) connecting the two detection points. Once enough lines of response are collected, reconstruction algorithms can infer the annihilation density at every position inside the body.

Time-of-flight (TOF) goes further by exploiting the difference in arrival times: the smaller the difference, the closer the annihilation point lies to the midpoint of the line. Modern all-digital PET pushes time resolution toward the hundred-picosecond scale, compressing the localization of the annihilation point to centimeters or less.

A map of metabolism

In clinical practice, injected tracers (such as F-18-FDG) accumulate in metabolically active tissue; the positrons they emit drift one to two millimeters in tissue before annihilating. The light of annihilation ultimately draws a map of metabolic activity in the body: the glycolysis of tumors, the oxygen consumption of brain regions, the viability of the myocardium, the edges of inflammation, all speaking outward in the form of 511 keV photons.

From tumor staging to neurodegenerative disease, from drug development to plant physiology, the light of annihilation 'sees the world' in more and more places.

Other stages of the light of annihilation

In materials science, positron annihilation spectroscopy (PAS) uses the lifetime and angular distribution of positrons annihilating in a material to reveal lattice defects and electronic structure, a sensitive tool for studying metals, semiconductors, and polymers. In proton therapy, In-beam PET is placed close to the treatment beamline and uses the annihilation photons of positron-emitting nuclides induced by the beam to verify the range online, providing feedback for precision radiotherapy. In cosmology, dark-matter annihilation may produce observable gamma-ray signals, and the Fermi satellite scans the sky with its large-area telescope, watching the most violent explosions in the universe.

The Light of Annihilation Continues

Look back along this thread: an equation, a cloud-chamber photograph, a pair of photons flying in opposite directions, a scintillation crystal, a digitized electrical pulse... In a hundred years, humanity turned 'annihilation' from a theoretical prophecy into an image outside the operating room. The most invisible light has become the clearest image.

At the Digital PET Laboratory, this chain continues to extend deeper: faster scintillation crystals, finer time resolution, digitization that stays closer to the physics, and single-photon-level sensitivity. Every electron-positron annihilation leaves a coordinate in the universe, and every advance in detection technology makes that coordinate more precise. The journey of the light of annihilation continues.
