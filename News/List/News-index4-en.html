Joining Forces to Turn Theory into Reality

The transformation of all-digital PET from a sampling concept into scientific instruments, clinical equipment, and an open research platform is not achieved through one-way 'technology transfer,' but rather through the joint efforts of universities, hospitals, enterprises, research institutes, government agencies, and international peers in defining problems, sharing evidence, and assuming risks at different stages. Collaboration does not begin as a final step; it starts when research questions are first identified.
September 9, 2026
Digital PET Laboratory
The journey of high-end scientific instruments from theory to reality is not a straight line. Physical principles must be transformed into manufacturable detectors, which then become stable whole machines, and these machines must enter real experiments and clinical settings. Errors encountered in applications must return to materials, electronics, and algorithms for correction. The value of collaboration lies in testing each type of knowledge at the place closest to reality and in distributing long-term risks that no single institution can bear independently.

Cross-Institutional Collaboration Underpins the Digital PET Laboratory's Success

PET is inherently a collaborative science. Radiochemists design and synthesize tracers, nuclear physicists and materials researchers select scintillation crystals, microelectronics teams read weak light signals, mechanical teams construct stable geometries, computational personnel reconstruct images from incomplete and noisy coincidence events, nuclear medicine physicians judge whether the images can answer clinical questions, biologists explain molecular mechanisms, and engineering and industrial teams ensure that instruments can be reproduced and maintained. The absence of any one component means that while the system may 'light up,' it cannot produce credible results.

All-digital PET further expands the scope of collaboration. MVT converts high-speed pulses into digital time coordinates, allowing more information to enter software; modular detectors permit geometric changes; PnI enables imaging protocols and algorithms to be combined. These degrees of freedom mean that teams must negotiate data interfaces, synchronization, calibration, and evaluation tasks earlier on. Collaboration is not about assembling completed parts but rather jointly deciding why components exist and how they are validated.

The Digital PET Laboratory has formed interdisciplinary medical-physics and medical-engineering teams since 2001, connecting different disciplines at Huazhong University of Science and Technology, affiliated hospitals, universities, and research institutions both domestically and internationally. Early collaborations with clinical PET centers allowed students to directly engage with medical scenarios, translating 'higher resolution' into lesion detection and quantification issues; cooperation with enterprises translated 'prototype operability' into consistency, reliability, cost, and service issues.

Collaboration also provides mutual constraints. Universities may focus on principles and papers, companies emphasize progress and products, hospitals prioritize safety and workflow, governments care about public value, and regulatory requirements demand traceable evidence. Different evaluation systems sometimes lead to friction but prevent any one party from substituting their local success for overall success. Truly mature medical instruments must simultaneously address scientific, engineering, clinical, and societal questions.

For the original route, cooperation is a risk-sharing community. In the early stages of all-digital PET, there are no mature products to reference, and basic research projects bear theoretical risks. Industrial partners take on engineering and market risks, while hospitals assume responsibility for clinical validation of new equipment. Local governments and public platforms provide transformation environments. If any party demands that another prove the entire value independently, the project will stall in the 'valley of death.'

The ultimate standard of cooperation is not the number of contracts signed but whether verifiable joint results are produced: a set of reproducible detection modules, a complete clinical dataset, a clear range error, a shared software interface, and a group of students who can continue working in different institutions. Only when these outcomes remain valid outside the initial team does cooperation transform from a relationship into capability.

Cross-Institutional Practice Shapes Collaborative Innovation at the Digital PET Laboratory

Hospital-Based Student Work Connects Engineering Metrics with Clinical Language

Early reports indicate that the Digital PET team maintains collaboration with the nuclear medicine team at Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. To bridge the gap between engineering and medicine, graduate students are sent to the PET center to participate in experiments. This arrangement, though seemingly simple, transforms how problems are articulated: time resolution in electronics is no longer just a scope indicator but must address image noise, lesion contrast, and scan duration; reconstruction algorithms cannot be optimal only in simulations but must also handle patient motion and real-world attenuation.

Collaboration with hospitals helps the team understand that 'correctness' in clinical settings encompasses safety, efficiency, and consistency. Patients cannot be kept for indefinite scans during debugging; technicians need clear procedures; doctors require interpretable results; and equipment must operate stably. Researchers bring these constraints back to the laboratory, driving improvements in detector modules, software interfaces, calibration, and fault diagnosis. Hospitals are not merely exhibition halls but collaborative research partners that pose questions and validate evidence.

Huazhong University of Science and Technology and the Suzhou Industry Team Productize Animal All-Digital PET

Key technologies for small digital PET imaging were jointly developed by the Digital PET Laboratory at Huazhong University of Science and Technology and Suzhou Rayence Technology Co., Ltd. The laboratory provided MVT principles, detectors, and imaging methods, while the company brought market demands, product engineering, and manufacturing standards to the project. Around 2010, both parties completed Trans-PET animal all-digital PET and further developed a series of products tailored to various research needs.

In related reports, Xie Qingguo points out that companies better understand user requirements and can steer instruments towards practical directions; stringent engineering standards also enable prototypes to achieve the stability needed for mature products. Conversely, university innovations in principles provide differentiated technological sources for enterprises. This collaboration is not merely about transferring patents to factories but involves iterative development and product refinement within a single feedback loop.

In 2013, 'Key Technologies for Small Digital PET Imaging' won the First Prize of Technical Invention Award in Hubei Province. More importantly than the award itself, animal all-digital PET entered the realm of biomedical research and drug development. Data generated by external researchers using the instrument, along with faults and new requirements, continued to serve as input for the next generation of detectors and software.

Ezhou Integrates a Principle Innovation into the Clinical Whole-Body PET Industry Chain

Human clinical PET requires significantly greater investment than animal systems. Around 2015, Ezhou City listed digital PET as an important technology transfer project and established industrial entities locally. The local platform, laboratory, and enterprise jointly provided facilities, funding, engineering personnel, and supply-chain coordination to complete the clinical all-digital PET prototype. Public reports also indicate that the core patents served as important assets for financing and valuation.

The significance of this collaboration lies in transforming one-time public support into a long-term conversion environment. The government does not replace scientific judgment or business operations but provides critical starting conditions for high-risk, long-cycle advanced equipment. Teams must respond to trust with verifiable milestones: prototypes, type testing, clinical trials, registration certificates, installation, and long-term service, each step more concrete than slogans.

In 2020, the clinical all-digital PET was put into operation at Ezhou Central Hospital. The hospital became a meeting place for research and development, industry, and public services: clinical use verifies reliability, real cases feedback optimize protocols, patients receive local high-end imaging services. From 'Project No.1' to actual operation, it demonstrates that regional collaboration is only completed in long-term maintenance and medical outcomes.

Clinical Trials in Guangzhou Convert Visibility into Evidence of Safety and Efficacy

Clinical all-digital PET is conducting clinical research and evidence collection at institutions such as the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. The hospital nuclear medicine team is responsible for subject procedures, image interpretation, controls, and clinical endpoints, while the equipment team handles system status, data quality, and technical responses. Both parties jointly translate engineering metrics into safety and efficacy evidence that regulatory bodies can review.

Brain-specific all-digital PET is also conducting hundreds of clinical studies at the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, involving Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, and Alzheimer's disease. Dedicated systems are not simply scaled-down versions of general equipment; detector geometry, head fixation, attenuation correction, brain region quantification, and clinical procedures all require revalidation. Clinical experts must participate from the initial project design to avoid discovering issues or mismatched real needs after equipment completion.

Collaboration with USTC and the Institute of Ion Beam Application Enables Proton-Beam Observation

In-line monitoring for proton therapy requires collaboration among particle therapy physics, nuclear reaction simulation, PET detection, and clinical procedures. The Digital PET team at USTC and related particle therapy institutions have conducted research on proton PET, developing dual-plate prototypes, energy transport observation devices, and in-beam PET solutions tailored to clinical use. The plate structure allows for the passage of therapeutic beamlets, while digital detectors preserve event information as much as possible under conditions involving short-lived isotopes and complex backgrounds.

In this collaboration, treatment facilities provide beamlines, plans, and clinical constraints, while the PET team provides detection and imaging. Research institutions such as USTC advance system development and applications. The research progresses from simulation to phantom studies, then to animal models and real beamlines, gradually assessing range errors. This demonstrates that imaging issues near large scientific installations require instrument teams to enter treatment environments and vice versa.

NEUROMED and International Researchers Transform Collaboration into a Joint Institution

Around 2017, the Digital PET team established an overseas research collaboration platform with the Italian Institute of Neurological Sciences (NEUROMED). Public records describe it as one of the research branches set up by the Chinese team in Italy. The cooperation focuses on brain science, whole-body all-digital PET for animals, and talent development. After installation at the local site, Italian researchers can conduct experiments using the shared platform, while technical teams receive feedback from different research environments.

Long-term international cooperation is more challenging than a single visit. Equipment maintenance, data standards, ethical approvals, student training, author contributions, and intellectual property rights all require cross-institutional coordination. The value of research branches lies in transforming collaboration from 'exchanging results' to 'jointly producing results': both parties take responsibility on the same instrument, with the same data and timeline.

The team has maintained academic connections with various institutions including the Finnish National PET Center, University of Chicago and Enrico Fermi Institute, University of California Berkeley, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Fermilab, CERN, and Moscow Engineering Physics Institute. The scope and duration of each collaboration vary; this article does not categorize them as joint projects but rather as an international network for particle detection, medical imaging, and talent exchange.

Cross-Border Collaboration Extends from Research Installations to International Clinical Services

Since around 2017, the animal all-digital PET has been applied in research institutions in Finland and Italy, bringing Chinese original technology into overseas research scenarios. Later, the industrial team advanced clinical full-digital PET cooperation with Mongolian medical institutions and proposed the concept of building a regional molecular imaging center. From exporting research instruments to collaborating on clinical systems, training, maintenance, radiopharmaceutical supply, regulatory compliance, and medical capabilities must be synchronized.

International installation is not just about one device leaving the factory but marks the beginning of a long-term service relationship. Power, network, language, quality systems, and clinical pathways may differ across countries; remote diagnostics and local engineering capabilities are equally important. Good international cooperation should help form independent usage and research abilities locally rather than relying on permanent external support.

Cooperation During the Pandemic Demonstrates the Team's Public Responsibility

In early 2020, the Digital PET team organized a relief action in Wuhan, communicating with hospitals to understand their needs and procure supplies. They completed equipment installation and debugging within a short period and stationed members to assist on-site. Xie Qingguo discussed with domestic and international medical teams the potential of using PET to study disease mechanisms, while other members provided frontline engineering support. The team later received the collective honor of the Hubei Youth May Fourth Medal.

Crisis cooperation reminds us that a technology platform is first and foremost a social infrastructure. Rapid response must be built on accumulated engineering capabilities, supply chain relationships, and hospital trust; at the same time, safety, ethical, and data standards cannot be relaxed during emergencies. A sense of responsibility only truly helps frontline clinical work when combined with professional standards.

Successful Cases Define Four Reusable Models of Collaboration

Principle and Product Teams Conduct Long-Term Collaborative Research

The experience of Trans-PET shows that technology transfer should not wait until the principle is 'completely finished.' The earlier enterprises participate in discussions on requirements and manufacturability, the less likely they are to need major redesigns later; universities must retain fundamental questions and independent verification to prevent short-term product goals from closing off technical routes. Both sides communicate with milestones, raw data, and joint testing rather than vague promises.

Hospitals Participate as Co-Designers

Clinical experience with all-digital PET and brain PET demonstrates that hospitals are more than just end-users. Nuclear medicine physicians, technologists, medical physicists, and ethics personnel should be involved in the protocol development, ergonomics, quality control, and endpoint design phases. The equipment team must also remain on-site for extended periods to understand the pre- and post-scan workflow of a single scan. Co-design elevates clinical trials from merely proving that a machine works to validating what problems it solves.

Local Platforms Organize High-Risk, Long-Term Translation

The case of Ezhou illustrates the role of local government, hospitals, and industry platforms in connecting original equipment. Public sectors can provide initial environments, demonstration scenarios, and resource coordination, but should be constrained by transparent milestones, third-party evaluations, and continuous service provision. For a locality, success is not just registering one company, but fostering talent development, supply chains, clinical capabilities, and public health services together.

Overseas Research Nodes Connect Equipment, Talent, and Scientific Questions

Collaborations with NEUROMED demonstrate that international platforms require shared instruments and long-term personnel rather than just signing agreements at meetings. Jointly training students, exchanging engineers, sharing governed data, and maintaining equipment together can create a genuine two-way knowledge flow. Collaboration outcomes should be attributed based on actual contributions and respect local ethical and regulatory standards.

High-Quality Collaboration Converts Shared Enthusiasm into Sustainable Systems

Define the Problem Clearly Before Discussing Resources

Collaboration intentions should start with a testable question: What process needs measurement, why existing methods are insufficient, what target subjects and time scales are involved, and how much main error is acceptable. Then decide on the required tracer, detector geometry, sample size, and computational method. If collaboration aims at acquiring equipment or publishing papers first, it can easily lose direction when real problems arise.

Jointly Define Milestones and 'Exit Conditions'

Exploratory projects allow for failure but require predefined criteria for continuing or changing course. These can be layered by simulation, bench testing, phantom studies, animal models, and human trials, with performance, data integrity, and safety thresholds defined at each level. Negative results should be recorded to avoid maintaining superficial success through constant metric changes. Collaborators should also agree on how to orderly terminate when resources are insufficient or evidence does not support continuation.

Place Data Governance Before the First Acquisition

PET data includes raw list-mode events, CT or MRI anatomy, injection records, clinical information, and algorithm outputs. Cooperation agreements should clearly define data controllers, access roles, retention periods, anonymization, cross-border transfer, secondary use, and deletion mechanisms. Training AI models especially requires agreement on whether the model can take away statistical features of the data, how to handle withdrawals, and how to evaluate population bias.

Intellectual Property Protects Contributions and Continues Collaboration

Background intellectual property should be listed at the start of a project, with clear attribution, licensing, and publication rules for independently generated and jointly produced results. Protection should not become a barrier to validation; openness should not ignore inventors' and implementers' contributions. Better mechanisms set different boundaries for research reproducibility, clinical use, and commercial licensing, while setting clear deadlines for manuscript reviews.

Ensure Clinical, Engineering, and Data Personnel Receive Proper Acknowledgment

In complex instrument projects, equipment maintenance, tracer synthesis, data cleaning, clinical coordination, and software infrastructure are often underestimated. Collaboration should adopt contributor role classification from the outset, documenting who proposes problems, designs methods, provides resources, maintains equipment, analyzes data, and writes papers. Accurate attribution is not only fair but also informs external readers about which capabilities the results depend on.

Establish a Common Quality Language

The meaning of 'good images' may differ across institutions. Projects need shared phantoms, standard operating procedures, calibration frequencies, software versions, and acceptance data; bridging studies should be conducted before and after equipment upgrades; cross-calibration is necessary for multicenter research. Any specialized systems beyond the standard must provide task-oriented performance metrics to allow partners to independently assess whether they meet study requirements.

Embedding Talent Development in Project Deliverables

If a device is usable only by the original manufacturer's engineers, it does not foster research capability. Joint projects should include joint mentoring of students, technical training, short-term exchanges, data analysis courses, and fault simulation exercises. Young members benefit most from experiencing the entire cycle from problem definition to paper or product, understanding adjacent disciplines rather than completing fragmented tasks.

An ideal kickoff meeting for a collaboration should at least address:What are the research questions and primary endpoints? What unique capabilities does each party bring? Are samples, equipment, radiopharmaceuticals, and computational resources available? Who is responsible for ethics and safety? How will raw data be preserved? How should failures and deviations be reported? How will joint achievements be attributed, shared, and translated?

A Collaborative Project Progresses from Initial Contact to Joint Outcomes

Discovery and Matching

Collaboration typically begins with an unmet problem rather than a ready-made contract. The laboratory first assesses whether the issue aligns with all-digital PET, PET imaging, nuclear radiation measurement, or related computational capabilities, then identifies missing partners. If the problem is inherently better addressed by another technology, this should be stated early on. The value of collaboration lies in complementarity rather than explaining every problem as a PET problem.

The initial discussion should protect both parties' undisclosed ideas, but the scope of confidentiality should not be so broad as to hinder normal academic work. Non-confidential concepts and public data can be exchanged first to form technical hypotheses; when it is necessary to exchange designs, patient data, or process information, a corresponding agreement should be signed. Trust builds gradually with clear boundaries.

Joint Design and Feasibility Assessment

Both parties should establish a joint problem statement that includes scientific background, primary endpoints, equipment and samples, timeline, ethics, data, intellectual property, and resources. The instrument team uses simulations and benchtop estimates to assess achievable performance; the application team verifies tracer feasibility, sample availability, and endpoint validation; statisticians review sample size and analysis plans in advance. A 'not ready yet' result from a feasibility assessment is also valuable.

The first milestone should be small but complete. For example, verify specific crystals with only one detector module, compare geometry on the digital phantom, test algorithms with limited anonymized historical data, or measure proton range in a standard phantom. Small experiments should cover the entire chain from input to evaluation rather than completing just an unvaluable component.

Project Initiation and Governance

A formal project requires a project leader, technical lead, data and ethics leads, and clear decision-making mechanisms. A joint steering committee handles direction and major changes; working groups address daily interfaces. Meetings are not measured by presentation length but by issue lists, decisions, evidence, and responsible parties. International cooperation across time zones should specify response cycles and document language.

The budget should correspond to the tasks, detailing equipment usage, isotopes, consumables, engineering support, computing, travel, and long-term maintenance. Funding from collaborators does not guarantee predetermined conclusions; laboratory-provided technology does not mean ignoring partner application knowledge. Conflicts of interest must be disclosed in papers, clinical research, and public communications.

Execution and Change Control

During project execution, raw data and experimental logs are shared facts. Each acquisition should be linked to equipment status, samples, operators, and software versions; problem reports document reproducible steps and resolution evidence. Critical design changes must assess impacts on existing data, ethical approvals, patents, and timelines; different versions cannot be used secretly at different centers.

The most common loss in inter-institutional collaboration comes from waiting: samples are ready but equipment is not calibrated, beam time is scheduled but detectors are still en route, algorithms are complete but lack geometry files. Joint timelines should mark these dependencies and set up advance rehearsals and alternatives. Project management here is not an administrative burden but a safeguard for scarce experimental opportunities.

Independent Validation and Joint Interpretation

Key results should be recalculated by individuals not involved in parameter tuning, with independent replication of some experiments conducted collaboratively. Clinical images should be evaluated using appropriate blinding methods and multiple readers. Algorithms should be compared to baseline methods on the same dataset, and negative results and excluded data should be documented. Joint interpretation meetings should distinguish between observations, model inference, and future conjectures, avoiding promotional language ahead of evidence.

If different disciplines arrive at different interpretations, there is no need to rush to consensus. New experiments can be designed to test hypotheses or uncertainties reported in the paper. One value of interdisciplinary collaboration is that the same result undergoes multiple theoretical tests; surface agreement may not necessarily be closer to the truth than clear disagreement.

Publication, Translation, and Long-term Maintenance

Before publication, all parties should confirm authorship, data status, and patent rights based on contributions, and prepare sufficient methods for external reproducibility. Publicly available code, phantoms, and metadata should have versions and licenses. If a product is formed, the technology transfer package must include testing, risk assessment, supplier information, training, and maintenance, not just prototype drawings.

After project completion, decisions need to be made about who will maintain equipment, data, and software, how security issues are communicated, how subsequent use is cited, and who takes over after students graduate. Long-term collaboration is marked not by projects never ending but by results remaining safely usable after personnel changes. Regular reviews should assess whether the partnership continues to serve both parties' missions, allowing relationships to naturally conclude at appropriate times.

The maturity of collaboration can be assessed as follows:Early on, look for common problems; mid-term, for shared data and evaluation criteria; later, for independent reproducibility of results by the other party; long-term, for platform functionality after initial contacts are gone.

Future Research Frontiers Require Global Collaboration

Whole-body Dynamic PET and Systems Biology

The long-axis whole-body system can simultaneously observe the tracer kinetics of multiple organs along the body axis. However, different tissue models, input functions, time delays, and data scales require a collaborative effort from medical physics, mathematics, physiology, and computational teams to address these issues. Cross-center sharing of dynamic reference data allows for parameter model comparisons and the establishment of normal population and disease cohort databases.

Brain-Body Coupling and Natural Behavior Imaging

Helmets PET, motion tracking, EEG, and behavioral experiments require a coordinated effort from neuroscience, mechanical engineering, detection technology, and ethics teams. Different laboratories can contribute wearable structures, motion correction techniques, tracers, and disease models separately, validating results across devices through common protocols.

In-Line Monitoring for Proton Therapy and Heavy Ion Therapy

This direction requires access to real beam lines and treatment planning systems. Particle centers, nuclear data teams, Monte Carlo researchers, PET engineers, and clinical medical physicists can collaborate to establish open phantoms, standard cases, and range error evaluations, allowing different monitoring methods to be compared under the same conditions.

New Radiopharmaceuticals, Microdosing, and Diagnostics-Integrated Therapeutics

The radiopharmacy center can provide new probes, while the pharmacology team studies metabolism and toxicity. The PET platform performs whole-body quantification, and hospitals conduct first-in-human and therapeutic response studies. International cooperation can expand rare disease and specific target sample sizes but must coordinate isotope production, GMP compliance, ethics, and regulatory requirements.

In Vivo Substance Transport in Plants and Ecosystems

Plant scientists can define carbon allocation, root systems, and stress-related issues. Instrument teams provide variable geometry, environmental platforms control light, temperature, humidity, and soil conditions, while modeling teams connect PET data with physiological information. Multi-site studies across different crops and environments can enhance the generalizability of conclusions.

Open Data, Open Source Software, and Reproducible Benchmarks

The data advantages of all-digital PET can only be utilized by the community when interfaces are clearly defined. Collaborating institutions can establish anonymized event datasets, digital phantoms, geometric descriptions, and challenges to evaluate pulse processing, low-count reconstruction, motion correction, and kinetic modeling. Public benchmarks also expose algorithm failures across different devices and populations.

Standard CMOS SiPMs and novel photon detection

Semiconductor processes, device physics, crystal materials, and system applications need to be optimized together. The photon detection efficiency, time jitter, dark counts, crosstalk, and radiation stability of devices must be balanced in real systems. Combining the process capabilities of industrial partners with the research team's mission requirements is necessary for new devices to progress beyond laboratory prototypes.

Research Institutions Worldwide Can Convert Shared Problems into Joint Projects

The Digital PET Laboratory is open for collaboration with universities, research institutes, hospitals, particle therapy centers, radiopharmaceutical platforms, companies, and public research institutions worldwide. We particularly welcome partners who bring clear scientific questions, unique experimental subjects, or complementary technical capabilities. Collaboration does not require prior experience in PET; life science and clinical issues themselves can serve as starting points for designing new instruments and methods.

If you study physical sciences, we can establish joint experiments around scintillation, single-photon detection, time measurement, radiation fields, and high-energy particles; if you study life sciences, we can design animal, plant, metabolic, immune, and neurotracer studies together; if you come from a hospital, start with unmet diagnostic, quantitative, or treatment monitoring needs; if you work in drug science, research the in vivo distribution, target occupancy, and dosing of candidate molecules; if you are skilled in mathematics and artificial intelligence, enter the complete data chain from pulses to kinetic parameters.

Collaboration can take various forms such as joint projects, co-developed instrument prototypes, commissioned research, clinical studies, joint graduate student training, visiting scholars, equipment and data sharing, standard and open-source software development, or starting with a small-scale feasibility test. For high-risk frontier directions, the first clear experiment often proves more effective than large agreements: identify an issue, a dataset, a common endpoint, and assess next steps within months.

We recommend that potential partners prepare a one-page research concept: problem and existing bottlenecks, target subjects, required time and spatial scales, samples or platforms available for provision, expected joint outcomes, and major ethical safety constraints. The laboratory will organize technical discussions among researchers in materials, detectors, systems, software, or application areas based on this concept to assess feasibility and the minimum validation path.

For human subjects, animals, radioactive isotopes, and cross-border data projects, collaboration must be conducted within corresponding ethical, radiation safety, data protection, and export control frameworks. For industrial projects, all parties should clarify background intellectual property rights, joint outcomes, licensing scope, and publication arrangements before investment. Rules are not obstacles to cooperation but the foundation for long-term trust among different institutions.

What we hope to achieve with our partners is more than just a paper or a device; it is an observational capability that others can continue to use: reliable detectors, reproducible data, open interfaces, validated methods, and the next generation of researchers who can cross disciplinary boundaries.

Global scientific issues do not arise according to institutional boundaries. Tumors, brain diseases, immunology, pharmacology, particle therapy, food and the environment all require different fields to share tools and evidence. The original innovation of all-digital PET originated in China, but the molecular processes it observes belong to shared human knowledge. We invite researchers from around the world to bring new problems to this platform and welcome rigorous experiments to test existing methods.

The true meaning of collaborative synergy is for each partner to maintain their professional judgment while being willing to open interfaces, clarify data, document failures, and accurately record contributions. Theory thus becomes more than just a possibility on paper, and reality is more than just a completed machine; both are mutually refined through repeated joint experiments, ultimately forming long-term capabilities that serve science and society.

Principles of Reciprocity in Collaboration

The value of partners should not be ranked solely by funding or equipment scale. Hospitals contribute real problems and clinical time, basic laboratories contribute unique models, developing regions may contribute different disease profiles and application environments, and students contribute long-term detailed experimental work. Project governance should give parties with fewer resources but irreplaceable knowledge a commensurate voice, avoiding marginalization of 'data providers' in the interpretation and attribution of results.

Global cooperation should also consider capacity building. When equipment enters new regions, it should be accompanied by training for nuclear medicine professionals, technicians, medical physicists, engineering maintenance personnel, and data analysts; after joint research concludes, local teams should be able to independently conduct routine work and raise their own questions. Only when knowledge becomes accessible can advanced medical technologies truly have global public value.

How to Judge Whether a New Collaboration Direction is Worth Starting

First, assess whether the problem is important and currently inadequately measurable; second, determine if all-digital PET or related pulse detection provides unique information rather than merely increasing technical complexity; third, evaluate complementary capabilities among partners; fourth, check if minimal validation can be completed within a reasonable timeframe and ethical risk; fifth, consider whether data and methods still contribute knowledge even if primary assumptions are not met. Meeting these conditions gives exploratory risks clear meaning.

Conversely, if samples are unavailable, tracers lack basic specificity, main endpoints keep changing, data rights cannot be clarified, or collaboration is merely for institutional name borrowing, then it should be paused before investment. Saying 'no' seriously protects research subjects, team time, and partnerships, while also reserving space for genuine cooperation when conditions mature.

The Digital PET Laboratory welcomes not just users of existing equipment but also those willing to challenge its boundaries. A difficult problem from neuroscience, botany, materials science, particle physics, or clinical practice may force detectors to change geometry, software to alter processes, and teams to establish new interfaces. It is precisely in this process of jointly facing the unknown that collaboration pushes theory towards reality and makes reality generate new theories.

We also welcome independent verification collaborations for existing conclusions. Trust within the community does not come from everyone reaching the same conclusion, but from data, methods, and boundaries being clear enough to allow different teams to test, question, and improve them. Technologies that can withstand such collaboration have the qualifications to become long-term research infrastructure.
