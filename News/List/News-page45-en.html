[Interviewee Profile] Nicola (from Italy)

Born in 1982. Joined Suzhou Rayence Technology Co., Ltd. in 2017 and settled in China. Currently a professor at Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Previously served as a researcher at the Future Linear Collider Department, German Electron Synchrotron Research Center, and an assistant professor at the Applied Mathematics Department, National Research Nuclear University MEPhI (Moscow Engineering Physics Institute) in Russia. His research focuses on the development and application of silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs), mathematical modeling, data analysis, parallel computing detection, etc. He has published over 230 papers in international high-level journals. In the top-tier physics journal Physical Review Letters, he has co-authored more than 110 articles as a joint first author.

The IEEE Nuclear and Space Radiation Effects Conference (IEEE NSS/MIC) is one of the leading international conferences in the industry. In October 2017, I represented China's digital PET team at the conference to present on the application of full-digital PET in precise proton therapy treatment, which was well received, earning our team three new talent awards. As a blue-eyed and blonde-haired Italian, representing China’s research team and promoting Chinese scientific achievements made people present somewhat surprised.

Actually, there's nothing strange about this because I am a 'New Chinese Person'.

I am Nicola, my Chinese name is Niujia, born in 1982 in a small city called Lametia Terme in Italy. In 2008, I received my doctorate from the University of Hamburg in Germany and subsequently worked at institutions such as DESY (German Electron Synchrotron Research Center), National Research Nuclear University MEPhI in Russia, and Oxford University in the UK. In 2014, I joined Suzhou Rayence Technology Co., Ltd. and settled in China.

If you want to change the world, come to China!

To talk about how my connection with China began, it all started from an accidental discovery during a sunny afternoon in Germany in 2010. In the University of Hamburg library, I came across a paper written by Professor Xie Qingguo's team at Huazhong University of Science and Technology in China, which developed the world’s first full-digital PET for animal use, achieving sub-millimeter high spatial resolution. As an 'insider', titles like 'first' or 'world-leading' are not uncommon to me; what truly caught my attention were some seemingly insignificant numbers: their team reduced the crystal size of the instrument to 1.89 mm and achieved a resolution of 1.00 mm. It was these small figures that left me utterly amazed—considering even Siemens, one of the global giants in PET technology, could only achieve a spatial resolution of 1.42 mm with crystals as small as 1.52 mm for their animal full-digital PET. Such technological breakthroughs made me realize China's unparalleled 'leading' position in digital PET technology. From then on, I remembered this team from the East.

In early 2013, I started working at National Research Nuclear University MEPhI in Russia. My team was engaged in research and development of next-generation photoelectric conversion devices SiPM (silicon photomultipliers). Theoretically, the industrial value of SiPM far exceeds that of PMT (photomultiplier tubes), which were widely used back then; however, due to technical bottlenecks, SiPM had remained confined to laboratories for over two decades without being applied in industry. China's success with digital PET technology gave me hope for the widespread application of semiconductor photomultipliers like SiPM: the technological framework of full-digital PET, especially MVT (multi-voltage threshold sampling) signal processing methods, could maximize the technical advantages of SiPM.

Due to a Sino-Russian national cooperation project, Professor Xie Qingguo and Professor Valery from my team got acquainted. Since then, our teams have maintained contact. In summer 2013, at Professor Xie's invitation, I visited Huazhong University of Science and Technology’s digital PET laboratory with Professor Valery. To our surprise, the first thing Professor Xie said to us was: 'When are you coming to work?'

'Shock!'—No other word could describe how we felt.

Our initial visit to China was meant for a tour and exchange; however, Professor Xie's straightforward invitation to join his team took us by surprise. We thought he must be joking. But then he clarified that he was serious. Although we did not immediately agree to his proposal at the time, we agreed on another arrangement: conducting 3-5 months of collaborative research annually.

In the following year, our teams frequently exchanged ideas. I became increasingly attracted by this passionate Chinese team; we are obsessed with scientific research and full of enthusiasm for our careers. We often gain inspiration and spark new ideas through exchanges. As a native Italian from the South, passion is my hallmark. In my mind, Professor Xie resembles an Italian Southerner—passionate and humorous, especially in his sense of humor. He would often joke after solving a technical problem: 'Do you want to hide in your lab writing papers for life or change the world? If you wish to change the world, come to China; solving China's problems is solving the world’s problems; contributions made for Chinese people are contributions made for all Earthlings.' On an unknown number of occasions when he cracked this joke over the phone, I suddenly said: 'I want to join your team and go to China.'

A year later, Valery and I both joined the digital PET team. Later on, Professor Xie often used me as an example for his team members, saying that joking is also a skill; if done well, it can really bring people in.

"New Four Great Inventions", Digital PET, 'Made in China' Changing the World

As soon as I arrived in China, some friends asked me if foreigners see China as a place where everyone rides bicycles, without flush toilets, poor and backward. Now, are you shocked? I wasn't shocked by the situation in China but rather by these questions — what misunderstandings do Chinese people have about us international friends?

Times have changed; for those of us who are part of Generation 80s, we can easily see huge changes on the internet: Shanghai and Beijing are as fashionable and modern as Rome, Berlin, and New York; products with 'Made in China' labels can be seen all over the world; Chinese people and their language can be heard everywhere at the top-tier universities where I once studied and worked. The 'new four great inventions' of China — high-speed rail, mobile payment, shared bicycles, and online shopping — have truly changed people's lifestyles.

Nowadays, you can order food directly on your phone and enjoy various delicacies without leaving home; I no longer worry about not having time to buy groceries or cook for myself alone, but rather whether my figure will change. I almost never carry a wallet because of Alipay, which means I don't need cash when going out; scanning a QR code with my phone allows me to ride bicycles anywhere. High-speed rail has greatly reduced the distance between people living on this vast land. I often joke with my Italian family that every time I return home, it feels like stepping back into 'the Stone Age,' as you can't go out without cash.

In fact, in my view, the 'new four great inventions' have changed people's lifestyles, but digital PET is a health tool that saves lives. In this sense, I believe digital PET is even more impressive than the 'new four great inventions.' PET (Positron Emission Tomography) is an important device for precision medicine and is known alongside CT (Computed Tomography) and MRI as one of the three essential pieces of equipment in hospitals; it's a powerful tool for early diagnosis of major diseases like cancer. Although PET, CT, and MRI technologies emerged simultaneously in the 1960s and 1970s, the widespread use of PET has lagged far behind these two due to technical bottlenecks related to ultra-high-speed scintillation pulse sampling. China's digital PET innovatively achieved precise digitization of PET ultra-high-speed scintillation pulses through MVP (Minimum Viable Product), eliminating analog circuits and achieving twice the result with half the effort, breaking a 40-year-old technological bottleneck that has made PET as widely used as CT and MRI, benefiting human health.

There's an Italian saying: 'Al bisogno si conosce l'amico' (Need is the mother of invention). Overcoming diseases like cancer, which were once considered incurable, is a need for all humanity. The Mediterranean Neurological Institute in Italy is one of Europe's largest brain disease research centers; it seems very large to me with 1,135 beds and thousands of cases annually. But when I came to China, I found that just in Suzhou alone, there are eight tertiary hospitals, each with a neurology department, handling hundreds of thousands of cases per year. This made me re-experience the 'joke' often mentioned by Professor Xie: 'The most challenging work is mostly done in China; solving problems for China means solving them for the world; contributions to Chinese people are contributions to all Earth's inhabitants.' The increase in cancer patients, immense aging pressure, and massive medical expenses have made early diagnosis, precise treatment, and new drug development urgent needs in China. PET is undoubtedly a key solution.

Doing meaningful work in China — that’s exhilarating!

While I see the significance of my work, I feel even more deeply the 'exhilaration' of doing things in China. 'Exhilaration' — a word I recently learned in China; it better expresses my feelings about working here than words like 'happy' or 'satisfied.' Because I'm working with very practical Chinese people. This is especially evident in the government's approach to problem-solving. In Europe, governments don't prioritize scientific and technological innovation issues, but for the Chinese government, science and technology are always at the top of their agenda; any issue related to innovation receives priority and efficient resolution.

In China, slogans such as 'Let innovation be the new engine driving development' and 'Focus on scientific progress and comprehensive innovation' can be heard daily on television. On October 18, 2017, we watched the opening ceremony of the 19th National Congress of the Communist Party of China (CPC), where the word 'science and technology' appeared 17 times in the report, becoming one of the high-frequency terms. China has proposed implementing an innovation-driven development strategy and building an innovative country; it can be said that China places a much higher emphasis on scientific and technological innovation than Europe.

China's emphasis on scientific and technological innovation is not merelyconfined to slogans. In 2017, China's total expenditure on research and development reached 1.76 trillion yuan, far exceeding that of other developing countries. Unlike the United States, where private sector investment dominates R&D spending, the Chinese government plays a significant role in funding research and development. From this perspective, departments such as the Ministry of Science and Technology and the National Natural Science Foundation are truly visionary 'venture capitalists'. They continuously discover good ideas, support researchers to conduct studies, and develop new technologies. Among them, the central government takes on the main responsibility for research and development, while local governments promote the transformation and application of scientific achievements. Collaboration between universities and enterprises, as well as university professors starting their own businesses, are all encouraged by the government. For example, in Suzhou, where I am familiar with, the entrepreneurial environment is very friendly. The government holds investment promotion meetings every year to help good technologies settle down, providing a series of training from recruitment to tax benefits and intellectual property rights, enabling those who work on technology to quickly understand business management.

China, thank you for making me who I am

In such an environment, I have more motivation and enthusiasm to devote myself fully to my career.

From 2015 to 2017, our team successively developed a digital PET detector based on SiPM (Silicon Photomultiplier), completed the research and application of high-sensitivity full-digital SiPMs, solved the current technical bottlenecks of SiPM, eliminated the obstacles that restrict the development of weak light detection fields from the root, promoted the leapfrog development of weak light detection industries and related sectors. China has provided increasingly favorable research conditions for young scientific workers like us, enabling us to focus on our research work.

Given my achievements in scientific research, in December 2016, the Italian government awarded me the title of 'Ambassador of Molise Region' (a region equivalent to a province in China), which is the highest regional honor in Italy, personally bestowed by the President of Italy upon scientists, cultural workers, and entrepreneurs living abroad. No more than four people are granted this title each year, and it remains valid for life. In fact, the nomination and election process was very long and complex; I never thought that my country would choose me among so many outstanding individuals. It is because of the opportunity to participate in China's digital PET research that my scientific dreams have been realized, which has ultimately led to this highest honor from my hometown.

In the new year, I will focus on studying the application of digital PET in precise proton therapy. Last year, during an experiment at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital in Taiwan, our team used digital PET for the first time to detect oxygen-15 produced when a proton beam hits human tissue. This suggests that the online monitoring challenge of proton therapy may be solved, potentially greatly improving its effectiveness in treating cancer. Digital PET-based proton therapy is one of the most cutting-edge technologies in the world today; its research and application are closely related to public health projects, and I am thrilled to be able to contribute to this challenging yet meaningful endeavor.

Another achievement that gives me great satisfaction is that in October 2017, my efforts led to the establishment of the Digital PET Molise Research Center. This center was jointly founded by Suzhou Rayence Technology Co., Ltd. and the Mediterranean Neurological Institute; it is the first overseas research center established by a Chinese institution within Italy. During the initial construction phase, the Italian government provided support in terms of personnel, facilities, and start-up funding. The establishment of this Digital PET laboratory aims to utilize Rayence's cutting-edge digital PET technology and products, combined with the Mediterranean Neurological Institute's rich clinical resources, to jointly conduct research on brain science, neurological diseases, and brain-like systems. Within 3-5 years, it is planned to establish a center for basic research on neurological diseases, new drug development, and innovative clinical treatment methods. I am particularly excited that on January 15, 2018, the Digital PET Molise Research Center will welcome its first outstanding Italian PhD student, Emanuele, marking the official start of operations at the center. As fellow Molisians, we all hope to work together in this center, exploring more unknown scientific frontiers, contributing back to our hometown and society.

What touched me deeply is that, in my previous impression, the cooperation model between China and developed countries in Europe and America was mostly characterized by Western investment in technology while attracting Chinese talent. However, now with the digital PET team, it's reversed: Chinese side exports technology and introduces foreign talent. In April 2017, Renrui Ning's animal digital PET was installed at the Finnish National PET Center; in June 2018, this equipment will also be installed at the Mediterranean Neurological Institute in Italy. When I saw a photo of an X-ray machine made by Italy and introduced to a hospital in Wuhan in 1914, I couldn't help but marvel that although I didn't live during the most glorious period of Italy, I have had the fortune to witness China's rapid development.

Now, with the global peak of digital PET equipment research and application forming in China, I am even more convinced that an increasing number of top talents from related fields will join us in China's digital PET research and contribute to China's wave of scientific innovation.

Just like Professor Xie often says: 'Our ultimate goal is to apply the digital PET technology to the health cause for the benefit of all humanity. It’s better to share joy with others than to enjoy it alone; any technological invention that concerns people's livelihood should be shared by Chinese people with the whole world.' This fully demonstrates a kind of confidence among Chinese scientists: they humbly absorb foreign talent while courageously sharing their own achievements. In these few years in China, I have noticed an increasing number of leading technologies like digital PET emerging like bamboo shoots after rain. I deeply feel that China is becoming more and more confident in science, culture, systems, etc., and it's this 'Chinese confidence' that gives us foreign scientists the courage and strength to achieve our careers and dreams here.

Such a belief also led me to make an important decision: to move my entire family to China to settle down as a 'new Chinese person'. In May 2017, my wife Daniela successfully defended her doctoral dissertation and is about to work in China. My four-year-old daughter and the soon-to-be-born little one will live and study in China; Chinese will be their mother tongue. I am also persuading my recently retired mother-in-law to come to China to help take care of the children. Of course, as a 'new Chinese person', I have some small worries. For example, due to nationality restrictions, I cannot apply for the National Natural Science Foundation of China's Excellent Young Scientist Fund project. Additionally, when applying for certain national research projects, one needs todefend the proposal in Chinese, and what makes me feel most 'inferior' is my language ability: after living in China for four years, I still can't understand or speak Chinese; my Italian English often leaves people confused; due to the language barrier, some of my suggestions have been rejected on the spot...

Despite the many challenges on the path to becoming a 'new Chinese person', I am fearless and full of anticipation.

I wish to be a Marco Polo of the new era

More than two thousand years ago, the ancient Silk Road connected China in the East with Italy in the West. Caravans laden with silk struggled across deserts, traveling between Chang'an and Rome; more than two thousand years later, airplanes and the internet have made the world a global village, with Beijing to Rome taking only 11 hours, and Chinese people needing just one press of a button to call Italians.

Today, China has developed into the world's second-largest economy, and the 'Belt and Road' initiative is leading the revival of the Silk Road. This road is not merely a passage but also a symbol of the 'Silk Road Spirit,' which embodies unity and mutual trust, equality and mutual benefit, inclusiveness and learning from each other, win-win cooperation, and the belief that countries with different races, beliefs, and cultural backgrounds can share peace and develop together.

As a scientist, I see the 'Belt and Road' as a path of intellectual convergence and innovation integration. I wish to be a Marco Polo of the new era, serving as an envoy for technological and cultural exchanges between East and West, dedicating my life's efforts to research on digital PET and contributing to the human health cause in fighting cancer and combating death.
